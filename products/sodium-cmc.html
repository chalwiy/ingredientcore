PRODUCT OVERVIEW

What is sodium CMC / cellulose gum?

Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose is the sodium salt of a cellulose derivative, commonly called sodium CMC or cellulose gum. These names identify the material family, not a particular viscosity grade.

Food manufacturers consider sodium CMC for thickening and stabilization in beverages, dairy and sauces. Match the grade to the formulation, hydration process and viscosity measurement method.

Also known as: Sodium CMC · CMC · Cellulose gum · Sodium carboxymethylcellulose

JECFA identity reference — material identity, not confirmation of an offered grade or destination-market approval.

GRADE & METHOD

Choose a CMC grade on matched test conditions

Keep identity separate from grade

Use the source’s full grade designation and current specification. Review purity, degree of substitution and physical form alongside the viscosity method; a grade code alone does not define those properties. Sodium CMC should not be confused with other cellulose ethers.

Resolve the concentration first

A viscosity result describes a specified solution and measurement. Match the concentration and its preparation basis, water, hydration time, temperature and instrument settings before comparing supplier data.

BUYER QUESTIONS

Sodium CMC purchasing questions

Is cellulose gum a different product from sodium CMC?

In this food-ingredient context, cellulose gum is a common name for sodium carboxymethyl cellulose. Use the full sodium identity and the proposed grade in purchasing records.

Can 1% and 2% viscosity values define one grade ranking?

No. Match concentration and its basis, solvent, preparation, temperature, instrument and settings before comparison. Do not infer one concentration from a different test.

Does matching solution viscosity make two grades interchangeable?

No. Compare the grade definition and full method, then evaluate the actual preparation and finished food. The same reported viscosity alone does not establish equivalent hydration, texture or stability in your formulation.

What helps investigate a hydration problem?

State the grade, water and salts, addition order, temperature, mixing, hydration time and when the defect appears. Keep lumping, viscosity development and finished-food separation as distinct observations.
