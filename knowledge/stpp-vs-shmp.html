STPP vs. SHMP: Identity, Specifications & Food Evaluation

Compare phosphate identity, reporting basis and process conditions before evaluating protein-water or mineral-management tasks.

INGREDIENTCORE BUYING GUIDE

Content revised 2026-10-03

STPP is pentasodium triphosphate; commercial SHMP is glassy sodium polyphosphate. Compare the exact grade, reporting basis and food process before testing either material. Similar phosphate percentages do not establish equal composition or an equal-weight substitution.

A change of reporting convention is not a change of chemical composition. Compare like-for-like fields; do not infer a replacement amount from two percentages. A pH result also needs solution concentration, preparation and temperature.

Define the food and mass stages

For meat, record protein source, salt, starting pH, brine preparation, mixing or tumbling, hold and cook. For seafood, also record species, treatment contact, draining and freeze/thaw history. Weigh at defined stages: treatment uptake and water retained after cooking or thawing are different observations.

The 1989 buffalo-meat and patty study tested named phosphates and blends in a specified salt system. Its abstract supports keeping the food and treatment conditions explicit; its numerical results and ranking are not predictions for another species, grade or process.

Reference: Original study abstract: buffalo meat and patties (1989)

Make the mineral-management question testable

If clarity or sediment is the concern, record the water and mineral ingredients, protein base, pH, mixing, heat treatment and when the problem appears. Compare the same preparation and hold schedule before judging a candidate.

Ask for the commercial material’s composition and dissolution information. A clear prepared solution alone does not establish stability in a finished drink.

Connect the sample with the proposed order

  1. Keep the current formulation and process as the benchmark.
  2. Have the technical team define candidate preparation, addition basis and any formulation changes.
  3. Request the current specification, relevant TDS, sample or representative COA and test methods.
  4. Identify the sample source and grade, then compare them with the proposed commercial offer.
  5. Review destination-market requirements and commercial terms separately.

Use evidence to make the purchase decision

Neither material is universally better. Make the decision against agreed food outcomes and source records. A chemical COA does not certify cooked yield or storage performance in your formulation.

For specifications, request the named analyte and basis: STPP compound content, phosphorus and P₂O₅-equivalent values must not be treated as the same percentage.

Buyer questions

Does “hexametaphosphate” mean a pure six-unit molecule?

Not for the commercial glassy material discussed here. JECFA describes a class of sodium polyphosphates, including material commonly called SHMP. Ask for the actual commercial composition and grade rather than treating the name as a molecular-purity specification.

Can I choose by the higher P₂O₅ percentage?

No. First establish the named analyte, reporting basis and method. Then evaluate the qualified material against the food objective. A higher oxide-equivalent report alone does not establish better texture, clarity or retained mass.

What should I request before comparing samples?

Request the current source-specific specification, relevant TDS, available representative COA and analytical methods. Record the sample identity and revision, and agree on preparation and food outcomes. Free-sample arrangements are discussed with sales; customer-required certificates are confirmed for the product, source, holder and scope.

Sources and scope

Use identity references, technical explanations and food-system studies for their stated purposes. Match each reference to its stated material, food system and test conditions.

  • FAO JECFA — Pentasodium Triphosphate

    Identity reference; not an offered grade or a market-use authorization.

  • FAO JECFA — Sodium Polyphosphate, glassy

    Commercial identity reference; not a source specification or reaction/performance result.

  • Original study — Phosphates in buffalo meat and patties (1989)

    Original study abstract: specified meat, salt and phosphate treatments. No ranking, dose or improvement figure is transferred to other foods or supplied grades.
